Is the crossing set by the alphabet or by the load?
===================================================

Script: experiments/g1_09_alphabet.py
Status: RUN on Actions, 25/25 cells. PREDICTION 1 REFUTED -- the crossing is
        flat in the alphabet too. BOTH hypotheses are dead, which this note
        called the most informative and least comfortable outcome. And
        reading the code afterwards showed why, and what the real load
        variable is.


IN PLAIN TERMS
--------------
Our rule stores things by layering them into one grid of numbers. The obvious
worry is crowding: the more you store, the more they smear into each other.

We tested that and it was wrong. Storing four things or sixteen made almost no
difference to how much room was needed -- and at small sizes, storing MORE was
actually BETTER.

The replacement guess was that what matters is not how many things you store but
how many DIFFERENT SYMBOLS exist to store. The symbols come from a fixed
alphabet that repeats in every sequence, so the crowding is regular rather than
random -- and a trained reader can learn to cancel regular crowding. What it
cannot do is tell apart two symbols that are not distinct enough in the space
available.

That guess has been sitting untested since. This is the test, and it is cheap.

It also matters practically. If room scales with the alphabet, then a system
meant to handle language -- tens of thousands of words -- needs to know how that
scales before anyone designs for it.


QUESTION
--------
g1-06 held n_keys at 32 throughout and found the crossing flat in n_pairs. Sweep
n_keys instead, at the tuned key_scale from g1-08 rather than the untuned default
that made the earlier curves unreliable.

  n_keys    8, 16, 32, 64, 128
  d_model   16, 24, 32, 48, 64
  key_scale 0.5 (best at widths 24-32 in g1-08; the crossing region)
  n_pairs   4, fixed
  5 seeds


PREDICTIONS -- written before the run
--------------------------------------
1. **The crossing GROWS with n_keys.** This is the hypothesis and the whole point.
   If it is flat, the replacement guess is as wrong as the one it replaced, and
   we have no account of what sets the crossing at all.

2. It grows roughly LINEARLY, or slower. Distinguishing keys is about their
   pairwise overlap, and d dimensions can hold d mutually distinguishable
   directions -- so d proportional to n_keys is the pessimistic reading. Random
   vectors stay nearly orthogonal at far higher counts than d, so sub-linear is
   quite possible and would be much better news.

   At n_keys=32 the crossing is 32 (g1-08). Linear predicts 8, 16, 32, 64, 128
   across the five settings.

3. **A confound is named in advance and cannot be removed.** Raising n_keys also
   raises the vocabulary, so the readout gets wider and there are more tokens to
   predict at every position, not just more keys to distinguish. The two cannot
   be separated in this design. If the crossing moves, "the alphabet sets it" is
   the honest reading only in the loose sense of alphabet-including-vocabulary.
   Saying so now rather than after seeing the number.

4. Sub-linear specifically at the top end: the jump from 64 to 128 keys costs
   less than the jump from 8 to 16 relative to the base. Random directions get
   cheaper per key as the space fills.

Most likely to be wrong: (2)'s exponent, again. The last two times this project
predicted a scaling law from a signal-to-noise argument -- g1-06's linear
interference law -- it was refuted outright. That is a reason to weaken
confidence in the exponent while keeping confidence in the direction, and to
treat a flat result as genuinely possible rather than unthinkable.


WHAT WOULD REFUTE WHAT
-----------------------
- crossing grows with n_keys  -> the replacement hypothesis survives; the scaling
                                 law is the deliverable
- crossing flat               -> BOTH hypotheses dead. Neither load nor alphabet
                                 sets the crossing, and we do not know what does.
                                 That would be the most informative outcome and
                                 the least comfortable
- crossing grows FASTER than
  linear                      -> bad news for the language goal: an alphabet of
                                 tens of thousands would need a prohibitive width
- crossing sub-linear         -> good news, and the number to quote for any
                                 vocabulary estimate


RESULT
------
5 seeds per cell, key_scale 0.5, n_pairs 4.

  n_keys     d=16     d=24     d=32     d=48     d=64
       8    0.703    0.882    0.932    0.995    0.997
      16    0.633    0.929    0.973    0.995    0.998
      32    0.562    0.875    0.975    0.992    0.999
      64    0.664    0.862    0.950    0.988    0.998
     128    0.626    0.843    0.940    0.983    0.995

crossing width (mean >= 0.9):
  n_keys=8 -> 32,  16 -> 24,  32 -> 32,  64 -> 32,  128 -> 32

**A SIXTEENFOLD CHANGE IN ALPHABET MOVES THE CROSSING BY NOTHING.** The 24 at
n_keys=16 is a single cell at 0.929 against a 0.9 threshold and is noise, not a
trend -- at n_keys=8, the smaller alphabet, the crossing is 32.


BOTH HYPOTHESES ARE DEAD
-------------------------
g1-06 refuted the load account: n_pairs from 2 to 8 barely moved the crossing,
and at small widths more was better.

g1-09 refutes the alphabet account, which was g1-06's replacement.

So the project has **no account of what sets the crossing**. That was named in
advance as the least comfortable outcome and it is the one that happened.


AND THEN READING THE CODE EXPLAINED IT
----------------------------------------
The store binds EVERY consecutive pair of tokens:

    if previous_key is not None:
        memory += np.outer(value, previous_key)

Every step, not only the key-value ones. So the number of bindings superposed
into the memory is **seq_len - 1**, not n_pairs.

    seq_len=96, n_pairs=2   ->  95 bindings, of which 2 matter (2.1%)
    seq_len=96, n_pairs=8   ->  95 bindings, of which 8 matter (8.4%)

Which explains both flat results at once:

  - n_pairs is flat because 2 against 8 out of 95 is nothing. The interference a
    query has to survive is 95 other bindings either way.
  - n_keys is flat because it does not change the count at all.

**So the interference account in g1-05 may have been right in mechanism and wrong
only in which variable carries the load.** It named n_pairs. The load is
seq_len -- which has been pinned at 96 for every sweep this project has ever run,
including the one that "refuted" interference.

That is a third distinct way to be wrong about the same question, and the same
shape as g3-02's: a variable held fixed everywhere, doing the work, invisible
because nothing ever moved it.


WHAT THIS PREDICTS, AND IT IS SHARP
-------------------------------------
If interference is the mechanism and seq_len is the load, then **the crossing
must grow with seq_len**, at roughly d proportional to seq_len by the same
signal-to-noise argument g1-06 made and had refuted for the wrong variable.

At seq_len=96 the crossing is 32. So:

    seq_len=48   -> crossing near 16
    seq_len=96   -> crossing near 32   (known)
    seq_len=192  -> crossing near 64
    seq_len=384  -> crossing near 128

That is g1-10, and it is the cheapest decisive test available.

If it is ALSO flat, then nothing about the task sets the crossing, the constraint
is intrinsic to the mechanism, and the honest position is that we do not
understand our own model. Worth saying now, before the run, so that outcome
cannot be quietly absorbed.


THE CONFOUND, AS PROMISED
--------------------------
Prediction 3 named in advance that raising n_keys also widens the vocabulary and
therefore the readout. Since the crossing did not move, the confound never became
load-bearing -- but it would have been the first thing to raise had it moved, and
recording that it was named before the result is the point.


A NOTE ON EXPECTATION-SETTING THAT WORKED
-------------------------------------------
This note deliberately weakened confidence in the exponent while keeping it in
the direction, on the grounds that the last scaling law predicted here from a
signal-to-noise argument was refuted outright. The direction was wrong too. That
is worth recording as a case where hedging was not enough -- the honest lesson is
not "hedge harder" but that a mechanism nobody has isolated should not be
generating quantitative predictions at all.
