Do tiny devices forget, and does clustering protect them?
=========================================================

Script: experiments/g6_02_tiny_forgetting.py
Status: RUN on Actions, 8/8 cells. **PREDICTIONS 1, 2 AND 3 ALL REFUTED.**
        Forgetting is governed by TOTAL width, not per-device width, so a
        cluster of tiny devices forgets no more than one large device of
        the same capacity. Clustering protects, and gating protects more.
        Prediction 4 held as the control it was written to be.


IN PLAIN TERMS
--------------
g7-02 showed that with selective storage, devices holding **one number** work at
every problem length tested, in clusters of a few dozen. That is the best news
this project has produced, and it is about a single body of data.

Real devices see more than one. So the question that decides whether any of it is
usable: **if a tiny device learns one thing and then meets a different thing, does
it keep the first?**

We already know large models here forget catastrophically below a certain size --
total loss at width 48, almost none at 112. What nobody has separated is *which*
size matters: the size of the whole network, or the size of one device.


THE CONFOUND g6-01 CANNOT RESOLVE
-----------------------------------
g6-01 varies total width from 48 to 128 while holding partitions at four, so
per-device width moves with total width, 12 to 32. Its answer is therefore about
both at once and cannot be split.

That confound is invisible in that sweep and fatal to the question this one asks,
because the two possibilities point opposite ways:

- **If forgetting is governed by TOTAL width**, a 240-wide network of width-1
  devices does not forget, and tiny devices are fine.
- **If it is governed by PER-DEVICE width**, every width-1 device forgets
  completely, a cluster pools 240 devices that have all forgotten, and tiny
  devices cannot hold two tasks at all.

Those are opposite conclusions about the central claim, and no measurement
distinguishes them.


QUESTION
--------
Hold total width at 240 and put every device at ONE dimension. Train on task A,
then task B, then re-test A -- at every cluster size.

  total width  240
  partitions   240, so each device holds one number
  cluster      1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 240
  gate         off and oracle
  lr           0.02, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2
  seeds        1, 2, 3
  phases       8 epochs on A, 8 on B; disjoint keys AND values
  measured     A before B, A after B, B after B

Cluster size is a read-time choice, so one trained model is measured at every
size -- 24 runs across 8 jobs. Costed: about an hour serial, worst job 7 minutes.

**Figure of merit: retained ABSOLUTE accuracy on A**, as in g6-01. A
fraction-kept ranking rewards whichever arm learned least.


PREDICTIONS -- written before the run
--------------------------------------
1. **Forgetting is governed by per-device width, not total width.** The delta
   rule updates each group from its own error over its own features, so a device
   with one feature has one number to overwrite and nothing to hold anything
   else in. Total width never enters that update.

   So width-1 devices should forget essentially everything, at every cluster
   size, and the retained accuracy on A should be near chance.

2. **Clustering does not protect.** A cluster pools members that have each been
   overwritten; pooling forgotten answers gives a forgotten answer. **If a
   cluster of 240 retains what a single device does not, prediction 1 is wrong
   and total width matters after all** -- which would be far better news.

3. **The gate does not protect either.** Selective storage acts on the memory,
   which is rebuilt every sequence; forgetting lives in the readout, which
   persists. Different mechanisms, so the gate should shift the *level* of task A
   accuracy without changing how much survives task B.

4. **Task B ends up fine in every arm.** It is trained last, so nothing has
   overwritten it. The connection control: if B is poor, that arm never learned
   and its retention number means nothing.

> **The control ran before dispatch and prediction 1 already looks wrong.**
> Gated, one seed, at lr 0.05:

>     A before  0.221  0.346  0.556  0.979  1.000  1.000  1.000  1.000

> Two things this does not yet settle. It is the GATED arm, and g6-01 measured

> 0.119 to 0.383, which is prediction 4 firing: those rows never learned the
> second task, so their retention numbers mean nothing and the summariser
> excludes them.

Most likely wrong: (2). It is the favourable direction and the one to distrust --
if clustering protects, the tiny-device story survives contact with multiple
tasks, and that is exactly the result this project would most like to see.
**Check before believing:** that A-before is high in the same arm, since a
cluster that never learned A cannot be said to have kept it, and that per-seed
values do not straddle.


WHAT WOULD REFUTE WHAT
-----------------------
- width-1 devices retain A          -> total width governs forgetting. Tiny
                                       devices survive task switching and the
                                       g7-02 story holds. Distrust, then check
- they forget at every cluster size  -> per-device width governs it. Tiny devices
                                       work within one body of data and cannot
                                       hold two, which bounds the whole approach
- retention rises with cluster size  -> partial protection; measure the curve and
                                       find the cluster size that suffices
- task B poor in some arm            -> that arm never learned and is excluded


WHAT THIS DOES NOT SETTLE
--------------------------
Two tasks, one switch, disjoint alphabets -- the sharpest possible version.
Real continual learning is many tasks with overlapping vocabularies, where losses
compound and sharing helps. Nor does it test rehearsal, replay, or any of the
standard defences; it asks only what the bare architecture does.

THE CONFOUND IS RESOLVED, AND THE ANSWER IS THE GOOD ONE
----------------------------------------------------------
g6-01 could not separate total width from per-device width, because it moved both
at once. Here per-device width is pinned at ONE and only the cluster varies.

  > **Forgetting is governed by TOTAL width, not per-device width.** A lone
  > width-1 device keeps nothing (0.000 of 0.114, ungated). Two hundred and forty
  > of them pooling keep 0.537 of 0.827. Nothing about the devices changed --
  > only how many were read together.

That removes the objection raised when g7-02 landed: that tiny devices might hold
a task within one body of data and lose it the moment another arrived. **They do
not, provided they are read as a cluster.** Retention rises monotonically with
cluster size in both arms.

**And the gate protects further.** At cluster 240, gated keeps 1.00 where open
keeps 0.65. At cluster 32 the gated arm has already stopped forgetting entirely.


THE PREDICTIONS
----------------
1. **Per-device width governs forgetting -- REFUTED.** It is total width. The
   reasoning was that a device with one feature has one number to overwrite and
   nothing to hold anything else in, which is true of the device and false of the
   ensemble: what survives lives in the *pattern across* devices, not in any one
   of them.

2. **Clustering does not protect -- REFUTED, decisively.** This was named before
   the run as most likely wrong and as the favourable direction. It was both.

3. **The gate does not protect either -- REFUTED.** The argument was that
   forgetting lives in the readout, which persists, while gating acts on the
   memory, which is rebuilt each sequence. The argument is sound and the
   conclusion is wrong: gating changes *what the readout is trained on*. With
   filler excluded, the two tasks drive updates through disjoint retrieval
   patterns, so their readout weights overlap less. The mechanism reaches further
   than where it acts.

4. **Task B fine in every arm -- HELD, as a control.** Below cluster 16 task B
   itself only reaches 0.040 to 0.410, so those rows never learned the second
   task and their retention numbers mean nothing. They are reported and excluded
   rather than quietly averaged in.


SCRUTINY, BECAUSE ALL THREE REFUTATIONS WENT THE FAVOURABLE WAY
-----------------------------------------------------------------
- **The gated headline sits at ceiling.** At cluster 32 and above, task A is
  1.000 before *and* after. "No forgetting" there is partly "nothing was hard
  enough to lose". **The open arm is where the claim has teeth**, and there
  clustering reduces forgetting from total to 35% lost -- a real effect, not an
  elimination.
- **The learning-rate grid pinned at its bottom in both arms**, all choosing
  0.02. Every number here is therefore under-tuned, and the ceiling results are
  the only ones tuning cannot move.
- **Per-seed spread is wide where it matters.** Ungated at cluster 240 the seeds
  give [0.442, 0.525, 0.644] -- a range of 0.2 on the single cell the open-arm
  claim rests on. Three seeds is thin for that.
- **One switch, disjoint alphabets.** Real continual learning is many tasks with
  overlapping vocabularies, where losses compound. Nothing here says they do not.
