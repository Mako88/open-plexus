Does a finite pool hold N constant, and does that recover anything?
==================================================================

Script: experiments/g8_03_capture.py
Status: RUN TWICE. The re-run at half-life 0.125 REFUTES prediction 1 and,
        with it, note 015's argument: bounding the lasting store cannot
        reproduce a mechanism that gates the FAST one. Details at the end.
        The first run, below, could not test its own hypothesis.

        First run: **PREDICTION 1 UNTESTABLE IN THIS GRID, because of a
        design error in the grid.** Half-life was pinned
        at 0.25 -- the value at which g8-01's recovery was ALREADY FLAT. The
        decline the whole sweep was built to explain lives at half-life
        0.125. There was no slope to flatten, so the central comparison had
        nothing to compare. Predictions 2 and 4 held; 3 is unresolvable.
        Every pool recovers approximately zero.
        Predictions were written before the script, before the controls and
        before any number from this grid was seen.


WHY THIS EXISTS
---------------
[Note 015](../../docs/notes/015-we-implemented-the-tag-and-not-the-competition.md)
found that the mechanism this project borrowed was implemented with its selection
step missing. Synaptic capture is **competitive over a finite pool** of
plasticity-related proteins -- tagged synapses contend and most lose. Note 010
took the tag and the later signal and gave the pool no limit.

The argument that this is not a detail is arithmetic:

    retrieval quality  ~  sqrt(d / N)

The oracle wins because it holds `N` **constant** -- filler is never written, so
the store holds `2 * n_pairs` bindings at any length. A **threshold** fires at a
rate, so promotions are rate x length, `N` grows with length, and recovery must
fall with length.

**[g8-01](g8-01-a-gate-without-an-oracle.txt) measured exactly that:** recovery
0.05 at seq 192 falling to -0.00 at 1536. It was written up as "the mechanism
fails hardest where it is needed most". Note 015 rereads it as "a threshold sets
a RATE and the oracle sets a QUANTITY, and no tuning of the bar converts one into
the other".

A fixed pool of `k` slots sets a quantity. That is the whole hypothesis.


WHAT IS BEING TESTED, PRECISELY
--------------------------------
Not "does capture work" but **"does holding `N` constant produce the
length-independence that is the oracle's whole advantage"**.

That makes the SHAPE of the recovery curve the measurement, not its height.
Recovery could stay flat at 0.02 and still confirm the mechanism does what it was
built to do while failing to be useful -- and those are different findings that
must not be merged.


THE ARMS
--------
  none            store everything, fade by decay              the FLOOR
  oracle          store only real bindings, read from the task the CEILING
  capture k=0     unbounded consolidation                      = g8-01's on-use
  capture k=4     a pool of four
  capture k=16    a pool of sixteen

The tag is confirmation-on-use throughout, so the ONLY thing varying across the
capture arms is the size of the pool. Salience is deliberately absent: note 015
separates tagging from capture, and mixing both dials in one grid would confound
which one moved anything.

`k=0` is not a control bolted on; it **is** g8-01's failing arm, so this grid
contains the result it is trying to beat.


THE GRID
--------
  seq_len     192, 384, 768, 1536     the same lengths as g8-01, so the two
                                      recovery curves are directly comparable
  k           0, 4, 16
  half-life   0.25                    pinned, mid-range from g7-04
  lr          0.02, 0.05, 0.1
  seeds       1, 2, 3

12 jobs of (seq_len, lr), five arms x three seeds each. Reported quantity is
recovery, `(arm - none) / (oracle - none)`, refusing to divide when the oracle's
advantage is not larger than the seed spread.


PREDICTIONS -- written before the script, before the controls, before any run
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. **Recovery for a bounded pool does NOT fall with sequence length, and the
   unbounded arm's does.** This is the hypothesis and the only prediction that
   matters. `k=0` should reproduce g8-01: about 0.05 at 192 falling to about 0.00
   at 1536. `k=4` and `k=16` should stay roughly flat across the four lengths,
   whatever height they sit at.

   **The comparison is within this grid**, between capture arms and the k=0 arm
   beside them, not against g8-01's numbers -- those were measured at a different
   half-life grid and are not a fair baseline.

2. **Recovery is still small.** Under 0.25 everywhere. Three independent
   mechanisms have now failed on this task and one architectural fix is a modest
   lever against that. Stated plainly because it is the unfavourable direction
   and it is what the evidence supports.

3. **There is an interior optimum in `k`.** Too small discards bindings that were
   worth keeping; too large is unbounded consolidation with bookkeeping. So k=4
   and k=16 should not be ordered the same way at every length -- and if one
   dominates everywhere, the pool size is not doing what the argument says.

4. **Control, expected to hold trivially:** at `seq_len 192`, where the oracle's
   advantage is smallest, the oracle still beats `none` by more than the seed
   spread. g8-01 measured that gap at 0.041 against a spread of 0.019, which is
   defined but not comfortable. If it fails, the shortest row is undefined and the
   length curve loses its left-hand end -- which would damage prediction 1
   specifically, since that prediction is about a SHAPE.

Most likely wrong: (1). Not because the arithmetic is wrong -- `N = k` really is
constant -- but because it assumes the thing limiting a bounded store is the same
thing limiting an unbounded one. If what actually caps performance is WHICH
bindings get in rather than how many, then holding the count constant changes
nothing and the curve stays flat at zero for a reason that has nothing to do with
the mechanism.


WHAT WOULD REFUTE WHAT
-----------------------
- flat recovery, bounded; falling, k=0 -> the note 015 argument is right about
                                          the MECHANISM, whatever the height. The
                                          first structural win against g8-01
- flat AND materially higher            -> a real gate exists. Best available
                                          outcome and the least likely
- both curves fall together             -> the pool is not holding N constant,
                                          which given the unit tests would mean
                                          the sweep is misconfigured rather than
                                          the idea refuted. Check before believing
- flat at zero for every k              -> N-constancy is NOT sufficient. What
                                          limits the store is which bindings get
                                          in, not how many, and note 015's
                                          argument is sound and irrelevant
- k=16 beats k=4 beats k=0 monotonically-> more is simply better, so the pool is
                                          acting as capacity rather than as
                                          competition, and prediction 3 is wrong


PRE-DISPATCH CONTROL -- and it already points at refutation
------------------------------------------------------------
One seed, lr 0.05, half-life 0.25, at the two ends of the length range.

    seq_len   none   oracle    gap   capture-0   capture-4   capture-16
        192  0.918    0.998  0.080       0.922       0.919        0.922
        768  0.697    0.998  0.301       0.690       0.690        0.686

As recovery:

    seq_len   capture-0   capture-4   capture-16
        192        0.05        0.01         0.05
        768       -0.02       -0.02        -0.04

**The bounded pools fall with length just as the unbounded one does.** That is
prediction 1 failing, at one seed and one learning rate, before the grid has run.

Two checks that the control is worth reading at all: the oracle's advantage of
0.080 at seq 192 matches g8-01's 0.081 at the same half-life, so the grids agree
where they overlap; and prediction 4's control holds, since 0.080 and 0.301 both
exceed any plausible seed spread.

**The sweep is dispatched anyway, and this is recorded before it runs.** One seed
cannot estimate a spread -- that exact mistake was made in g8-01's control, which
declared seq 192 vacuous on a single-seed gap and was wrong once three seeds
tightened it. And a pre-registered prediction is worth testing properly even when
a cheap probe has already leaned on it, because the alternative is a project that
only finishes the experiments it expects to win.

What this control does NOT do is distinguish the two failure modes named in the
refutation table: whether the pool fails to hold `N` constant (a misconfiguration)
or holds it constant to no benefit (N-constancy is insufficient). The slope
column across four lengths and three seeds is what separates those.


WHAT THIS CANNOT SETTLE
------------------------
**Whether the ranking signal is any good.** Strength here is the norm of the
trace being promoted, chosen because it is local, cheap and arm-agnostic. It is
not argued to be the right ranking, only an available one. A null result leaves
open that a better ranking over the same pool would work, and that is a separate
sweep.

**Anything about replay.** The pool still decides at storage time, using only
what is known then. An offline phase that revisits traces later is a different
and more promising mechanism, recorded in BACKLOG.md and untouched here.

**Whether `k` transfers.** The right pool size on a task with four pairs is
almost certainly not the right size on real data, and nothing here says how it
scales.


RESULT
------
Recovery by pool size and sequence length, half-life 0.25, three seeds, each
length at its best learning rate.

        pool      192      384      768     1536    slope
   capture-0     0.00     0.01    -0.02    -0.00    -0.01
   capture-4    -0.01    -0.02    -0.01    -0.00     0.01
  capture-16     0.00     0.00    -0.02    -0.00    -0.01

Every cell is within 0.02 of zero. Nothing recovers anything.


THE DESIGN ERROR, WHICH IS THE MAIN FINDING
---------------------------------------------
`capture-0` is unbounded consolidation -- g8-01's failing arm -- and it was put in
this grid precisely so the comparison would be internal. Its row reads
0.00 / 0.01 / -0.02 / -0.00.

g8-01's recovery table, at half-life 0.25:

    192   0.25    0.00
    384   0.25    0.01
    768   0.25   -0.02
   1536   0.25   -0.00

**Identical.** Which is a genuine cross-validation -- two grids, different code
paths, an arm-spec refactor in between, and they agree to three decimals where
they overlap -- and is also the problem.

The decline that motivated this entire sweep, 0.05 at seq 192 falling to -0.00 at
1536, comes from g8-01's **half-life 0.125** row:

    192   0.125   0.05
    384   0.125   0.04
    768   0.125  -0.00
   1536   0.125  -0.01

**This grid pinned half-life at 0.25, which is where recovery was already flat.**
Prediction 1 asks whether a bounded pool flattens a curve that falls. In this
grid nothing falls, including the unbounded arm it was supposed to be contrasted
against. There is no slope to remove, so a flat result says nothing about whether
the pool would have removed one.

The pre-registration named the pinning and defended it: "this asks whether the
exponent moves recovery at ONE operating point". That defence was about the
learning rate and the length. **Nobody checked which operating point the effect
being explained actually lived at**, and the answer was: not this one.

**This is the sweep-that-does-not-contain-its-own-answer failure, arrived at from
a new direction.** The grid was checked for having enough range in the axes it
swept and never checked for whether the axis it froze was the load-bearing one.


THE PREDICTIONS
----------------
1. **A bounded pool does not fall with length while k=0 does -- UNTESTABLE.**
   Not refuted: untested. Both arms are flat because the configuration has no
   length dependence to begin with. Scoring this as a refutation would be
   claiming a measurement that was not made.

2. **Recovery is still small, under 0.25 everywhere -- HELD**, and by a wide
   margin. Every cell is within 0.02 of zero.

3. **There is an interior optimum in k -- UNRESOLVABLE.** capture-4 and
   capture-16 differ by 0.00 to 0.02 against seed spreads far larger. No ordering
   is measurable.

4. **Control: the oracle beats none by more than the seed spread -- HELD** in
   every cell, so no row is undefined.


WHAT IT DOES ESTABLISH
-----------------------
A bounded pool recovers nothing at half-life 0.25, at four lengths and three
learning rates. That is a real negative result at one operating point, and it
adds a fifth mechanism-configuration to the four already failing.

It does **not** establish that N-constancy fails to do what note 015 argues,
because the argument is about removing a length-dependence and this grid has
none.


WHAT HAPPENS NEXT
------------------
Re-run pinned at **half-life 0.125**, where the decline exists. Same script, same
arms, one changed constant: 12 jobs, and the contrast prediction 1 needs is
present by construction.

Recording the order: this correction was identified from the returned data, not

RE-RUN AT HALF-LIFE 0.125 -- PREDICTION 1 REFUTED, AND NOTE 015 IS WRONG
=========================================================================
Same arms, same lengths, one changed constant. The contrast prediction 1 needed
is present this time: capture-0 reproduces g8-01's decline exactly.

        pool      192      384      768     1536    slope
   capture-0     0.05     0.04    -0.00undefined      n/a
   capture-4     0.02     0.01    -0.01undefined      n/a
  capture-16     0.05     0.04    -0.01undefined      n/a

(1536 is undefined for every pool because its floor arm is below the trivial
floor -- the summariser now refuses those cells rather than preferring them.)

**Every curve falls.** The unbounded arm falls 0.05 to -0.00, which is the
decline note 015 set out to explain. The bounded pools fall too: 0.02 to -0.01
and 0.05 to -0.01. Holding `N` constant did not flatten anything.

THE PRE-REGISTERED CHECK, WHICH IS WHY THIS IS A REFUTATION AND NOT A MYSTERY
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The refutation table said: *"both curves fall together -> the pool is not holding
N constant, which given the unit tests would mean the sweep is misconfigured
rather than the idea refuted. Check before believing."*

Checked. The pool **is** binding: capture-4 differs from capture-0 at every
length (0.02 against 0.05 at seq 192), and `tests/test_competitive_capture.py`
pins that a pool larger than the demand reproduces unbounded consolidation
*exactly* while a small one does not. The mechanism does what it says.

So this is the other pre-registered branch: **N-constancy is not sufficient.**

WHY -- AND THE ARGUMENT HAS A HOLE IN IT
------------------------------------------
Note 015 reasoned: retrieval goes as `sqrt(d / N)`, the oracle wins by holding
`N` constant, a threshold lets `N` grow with length, so bound `N` and the
length-dependence goes away.

**It confused two different stores.**

The oracle is `run(store=mask)`. It gates the **fast store** -- filler is never
written into `memory` at all, so the fast store holds `2 * n_pairs` bindings
whatever the sequence length. That is where its `N` is held constant, and that is
the whole of its advantage.

Competitive capture bounds the **lasting store**. It has no effect on the fast
store, which still accumulates one binding per step regardless. So `N_fast` grows
with sequence length exactly as before, retrieval degrades exactly as before, and
a bounded lasting store is a small additive term on top of a memory that is
already saturating.

**Capping the lasting store cannot reproduce a mechanism that gates the fast
one.** The arithmetic in note 015 is correct and is about the wrong `N`.

THE PREDICTIONS
----------------
1. **Bounded pools stay flat while k=0 falls -- REFUTED.** All three fall, and by
   similar amounts. The pool binds, so this is a refutation of the argument
   rather than of the implementation.

2. **Recovery under 0.25 everywhere -- HELD.** Maximum 0.05.

3. **An interior optimum in k -- REFUTED, and informatively.** capture-16 tracks
   capture-0 almost exactly (0.05/0.04/-0.01 against 0.05/0.04/-0.00) while
   capture-4 is *worse* than both (0.02/0.01/-0.01). A larger pool behaves like
   no pool; a smaller one discards useful bindings. Monotone in the unhelpful
   direction, with no interior optimum.

4. **Control: the oracle beats none by more than the seed spread -- HELD** at
   192, 384 and 768; undefined at 1536 for the floor reason above.

WHAT THIS COSTS AND WHAT IT BUYS
----------------------------------
Costs: competitive capture is a sixth failed mechanism, and note 015's central
argument is withdrawn.

Buys: **the first clear statement of what the oracle actually does.** It is not
"selective consolidation". It is *gating admission to the fast store*, and no
mechanism tried so far has attempted that -- every one of the six operates on the
lasting store or on what gets promoted out of the fast one.

That reframes what to try next. A gate that could work has to decide, at write
time, whether a binding enters `memory` at all. `run(store=...)` already has that
shape; the open problem has always been what fills the mask, and note 016 argues
the signal has to come from outside the statistics of the stream. The
reward-recall task exists to supply exactly that, and
[g9-02](g9-02-a-gate-that-reads-its-own-input.txt) is pre-registered to test it
**as a fast-store gate**, which is now the only version worth testing.
