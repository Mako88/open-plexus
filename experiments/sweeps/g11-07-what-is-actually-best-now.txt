What combination is actually best, now that the readout has changed?
====================================================================

Script: experiments/g11_04_scaling_exponent.py, --components spec per cell
Status: DISPATCHED, awaiting results.


IN PLAIN TERMS
---------------
Over the last day we changed the part of the model that turns its internal state
into a guess — and then found that several things we had previously measured were
only helping because that part was weak. Sparse keys helped a weak guesser and
hurt a strong one. The exact cache helped a weak guesser a lot and a strong one
barely.

**So we no longer know which combination of parts is best.** Every comparison in
the record was made against the old guesser. This runs all the combinations at
once and produces one table, instead of finding out one surprise at a time.


WHY THIS EXISTS
----------------
Decision 74 established that **every number in this project's comparison set was
measured beside a LINEAR readout**, and decision 75 put a composed one in the
model. Decision 76 was the first re-check and it moved a headline result: the
cache's advantage at equal state fell from 0.236 bits to 0.060.

Re-checking one mechanism at a time produces a stream of surprises. A grid
produces a table. CLAUDE.md now carries the rule this follows — *a measurement is
conditional on the configuration it was taken in; re-validate when the condition
moves*, and re-check in order of blast radius.

**The refuted mechanisms are in the grid on purpose.** `pair` keys were measured
0.23 bits worse and `settle2` was refuted at 0.924 -> 0.128, both against a linear
readout. **A mechanism refuted for adding information a linear map could not use
is not refuted for a readout that can**, and leaving them out would re-validate
only the half of the record that currently looks good.


PREDICTIONS
------------
Registered before dispatch.

  P1  IDENTITY. `keys=dense,retrieval=plain,readout=linear` lands near 5.53 at
      60,000 characters, which is where it sat in three separate probes today.
      Disagreement is a dispatch error, not a finding.

  P2  THE READOUT DOMINATES. `readout=hidden128` wins at EVERY combination of
      keys and retrieval. Nothing else measured today moves the loss as much.

  P3  THE CACHE SHRINKS EVERYWHERE. Its advantage under `hidden128` is smaller
      than under `linear` at every keys setting — decision 76 measured that at
      one setting and this asks whether it generalises.

  P4  THE SPARSE CROSSOVER REPLICATES. `sparse4` beats `dense` under `linear`
      and loses to it under `hidden128`, reproducing decision 74 at 60,000
      characters rather than 120,000.

  P5  **PAIR KEYS RECOVER, AT LEAST PARTLY.** Against `dense`, the pair-key
      penalty is SMALLER under `hidden128` than under `linear`. This is the one
      genuinely open prediction and the reason the grid includes refuted arms:
      a pair key's ceiling is a trigram rather than a bigram, so it carries more
      information, and note 034 measured it winning a two-token task 0.533 ->
      1.000 while losing on the corpus. **Losing while carrying more information
      is exactly what a linear readout would produce.**

WHAT WOULD REFUTE EACH: P1 by a baseline away from ~5.53. P2 by any cell where
linear wins. P3 by the cache gap widening under the composed readout anywhere.
P4 by sparse winning or losing under both. P5 by the pair-key penalty being the
same or larger under `hidden128`.

**P5 is the one worth the matrix.** If pair keys recover, the refutation was
about the readout and not about the mechanism, and every other refutation in the
record is suspect in the same way. If they do not, the pair-key result stands on
its own and the re-validation can be narrowed.


COST
-----
18 jobs: 3 keys x 3 retrieval x 2 readouts, two seeds inside each on 2 workers,
width 64, chunk 128, 60,000 characters.

    keys        dense, sparse4, pair
    retrieval   plain, cache128, settle2
    readout     linear, hidden128

Measured locally at 60,000 characters, one seed:

    keys=dense,retrieval=plain,readout=linear         18.1 s   (cheapest)
    keys=pair,retrieval=cache128,readout=hidden128    45.4 s   (most expensive)

From the MOST EXPENSIVE CELL per g11-03's rule, and using the **7.6x
local-to-runner factor measured on g11-06** rather than a guess: 45.4 s per seed,
two seeds on two workers, so ~6 minutes of wall clock against a 300-minute
timeout. The whole matrix is roughly 18 minutes of local-equivalent job time.

**This is a cheap grid and that is the point.** It costs less than the single
g11-06 cell that ran for 190 minutes, and it answers a question about eighteen
combinations rather than one.


MEASURED ON
------------
Tiny Shakespeare, 64 symbols, 60,000 training characters. **Deliberately the
same protocol as the record being re-validated** — 80/20 fit/calibration split
within the stream, temperature fitted on held-out training text, tested on the
corpus's held-out tail, two epochs. Changing the protocol at the same time as the
readout would confound the thing this grid exists to isolate.

C4 asks for prequential scoring on a single pass, and every number here is a
split. That is a known and deliberate limitation: **this grid re-validates the
old comparison set on its own terms.** Moving to prequential is a separate change
and will need its own re-validation.

60,000 characters is above the ~16,000 saturation point (decision 63), so this
grid measures LEVEL and says nothing about slope. That is what it is for.

Arms are named by composition — `keys=X,retrieval=Y,readout=Z` — so two different
models can never share a name, which `--mode` allowed and which g11-06 had to
work around with a duplicate arm.
