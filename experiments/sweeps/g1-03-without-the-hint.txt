Does the objective still bootstrap without the architectural hint?
==================================================================

Script: experiments/g1_03_no_hint.py
Status: RUN, 5 seeds then 20. Predictions 1, 2 and 4 held. Prediction 3
        REFUTED -- failure is not graded, it is BIMODAL, and adding candidates
        makes it LESS likely to fail rather than more.


IN PLAIN TERMS
--------------
Last time, a model taught itself our memory task perfectly just by predicting
what came next. But we had quietly helped it: its wiring was built so that
"look back at where you saw this before, and report what came NEXT" was the
natural thing for it to do. We handed it the shape of the answer.

This takes the hint away by degrees. Instead of being wired to look one step
ahead, the model gets several candidate places to look and has to work out for
itself which one is useful.

If it still succeeds, the earlier result was about the learning method rather
than about our helpful wiring. If it fails, we learn how much of that result we
were supplying ourselves -- which is worth knowing before building anything on
top of it.


QUESTION
--------
g1-02 hardcoded the value source to offset +1: attending to position s retrieved
the token at s+1. That is the induction shape, given rather than learned.

`value_offsets` now makes it a learned mixture. Conditions:

  (1,)              the hint, exactly as g1-02. Control.
  (0, 1)            two candidates. Must learn to prefer +1 over "read the token
                    you attended to" -- the single most confusable alternative.
  (-1, 0, 1)        three, including a backwards read.
  (-1, 0, 1, 2)     four. +2 is causally masked further back, so choosing it
                    costs reach as well as being wrong.
  (-2,-1, 0, 1, 2)  five.

Objective is `all` -- pure next-token prediction over every position, random
filler, exactly the configuration that reached 1.000 in g1-02. 5 seeds each.


PREDICTIONS -- written before the run
--------------------------------------
1. (1,) reproduces 1.000. It is the same model g1-02 ran, reached through the
   new mixture code with a single weight near 1.0. If it does not reproduce, the
   refactor changed behaviour and the whole comparison is void. This is the
   connection control.

2. (0, 1) still reaches high, above 0.9. Two candidates, one useful, and the
   gradient signal distinguishing them is strong -- offset 0 retrieves the key
   itself, which predicts nothing, while +1 retrieves the answer.

3. Accuracy falls as candidates are added, but slowly. Guess: ~1.0, ~0.95, ~0.9,
   ~0.85 across the five conditions. The search is over a handful of scalars, not
   a hard combinatorial problem.

4. The learned mixture ends up concentrated on +1 in every successful run. This
   is the mechanistic check: a high score with the weight NOT on +1 would mean
   the model found some other route and the interpretation is wrong.

Most likely to be wrong: (3). Two ways it could fail that are worth separating
in advance --
   (a) accuracy collapses immediately at (0,1), which would mean g1-02's result
       was mostly the hint and the objective is weaker than it looked;
   (b) accuracy holds at 1.000 for every condition, which would mean the mixture
       is trivially easy to learn and this experiment was not a real test. If so
       the honest follow-up is a harder relaxation -- a second layer, so the
       shift has to be composed rather than selected.


WHAT WOULD REFUTE WHAT
-----------------------
- (1,) not 1.000            -> refactor broke something; discard the table
- (0,1) at the floor        -> g1-02's result was substantially the hint
- all conditions at 1.000   -> the relaxation was too easy to count as removing
                               the hint; needs a harder version before claiming
- high score with mixture   -> the model is solving it some other way and our
  weight not on +1             mechanistic story is wrong



RESULT
------
5 seeds per condition:

value_offsets       query acc          spread  argmax offset
-------------------------------------------------------------
(1,)                    1.000       all equal   5/5 chose +1
(0, 1)                  0.809     0.045-1.000   3/5 chose +1
(-1, 0, 1)              1.000       all equal   5/5 chose +1
(-1, 0, 1, 2)           1.000       all equal   5/5 chose +1
(-2, -1, 0, 1, 2)       1.000       all equal   5/5 chose +1

The (0,1) spread was 0.045-1.000, so the mean is describing a mixture of two
populations rather than a typical run. Re-run at 20 seeds on the three
conditions that matter, reporting every seed:

offsets=(1,)         mean 1.000   solved 20/20   stuck  0/20   between  0/20
     1.00 x20
offsets=(0, 1)       mean 0.615   solved 12/20   stuck  8/20   between  0/20
     0.03 0.03 0.04 0.04 0.04 0.04 0.04 0.05 1.00 1.00 1.00 1.00 1.00 1.00
     1.00 1.00 1.00 1.00 1.00 1.00
offsets=(-1, 0, 1)   mean 0.951   solved 19/20   stuck  1/20   between  0/20
     0.02 1.00 x19


WHAT HELD
---------
1. (1,) reproduces 1.000 at 20/20. The refactor from a hardcoded shift to a
   learned mixture did not change behaviour, so the comparison is valid.

2. The hint is NOT what produced g1-02's result. Every relaxation still solves
   the task in the majority of runs, and three of the four solve it in 19/20 or
   better. **The objective finds the task without being told where to look.**

4. The learned mixture concentrates on +1 in every successful run, across every
   condition. The mechanistic story holds: the model is doing induction, not
   finding some other route.


REFUTED, AND THIS IS THE FINDING
---------------------------------
3. Predicted a gradual decline as candidates were added: ~1.0, ~0.95, ~0.9,
   ~0.85. Both halves are wrong.

**FAILURE IS BIMODAL, NOT GRADED.** Across 60 runs there is not one intermediate
outcome. Every run is either ~1.00 or ~0.04. The "between" column is 0/20 in
every condition. A run either finds the induction solution completely or never
gets near it.

**AND MORE CANDIDATES IS NOT HARDER.** The failure rate is non-monotonic:

    (1,)          0/20 stuck
    (0, 1)        8/20 stuck     <- the worst condition, with the FEWEST choices
    (-1, 0, 1)    1/20 stuck

Adding a third candidate that is useless on its own -- offset -1, reading the
token before -- takes the failure rate from 40% to 5%. That is the opposite of
the prediction and it is not a small effect.

**The mechanism is unknown and is recorded as unknown.** A plausible story is
that offset 0 -- retrieving the key you attended to -- is a plausible-looking
attractor that predicts nothing, and that with only two candidates a run
initialised toward it cannot escape. That is a story, not a measurement, and it
should not be written into the code as if it were understood.


WHAT THIS SETTLES THAT WAS PREVIOUSLY UNSUPPORTED
--------------------------------------------------
docs/notes/008 §2 struck a claim from an earlier summary -- that the task "has a
step, not a slope" -- on the grounds that it was a statement about optimisation
landscapes and nothing had measured one. That was the right call at the time.

**Something has now been trained, and the step is real.** 60 runs, two outcomes,
nothing between them. Partial retrieval buying partial prediction -- the slope
the note argued probably existed in output space -- is not what training
traverses. Training either lands in the basin or it does not.

The note's correction stands as written: the claim was unsupported when made.
It is supported now, by the experiment the note said would be required.


WHAT IT MEANS FOR G1
---------------------
This changes the question G1 should ask, and the change is worth acting on.

The plan was: design a local rule, measure how close it gets to 1.000. With
bimodal outcomes that framing is wrong -- an average over seeds describes a
mixture of two populations and no individual run, exactly as the (0,1) mean of
0.615 does above.

**G1 should measure HOW OFTEN a rule finds the solution, not how close it gets.**
Report solved/stuck counts, not means. A local rule that solves it 3 times in 20
is a very different result from one that reaches 0.15 every time, and a mean
would render them identical.

And a caution for expectations: this is a backprop learner with global
information, and it still fails 40% of the time in one configuration. A local
rule has strictly less to work with.
