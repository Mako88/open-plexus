Does a per-surface bound fix the star?
======================================

Script: experiments/g33_04_does_a_derived_bound_fix_the_star.py
Status: RUN, local, 2026-07-31, 29.0s. Predictions committed at `5a214a4`
        before the script was executed.

ANSWER: **the derived bound is the best arm in eight of nine cells and collapses
        in none.** It turns the star from unusable into workable — 0.1667 to
        0.8429 bridged at five surfaces, with f1 0.4847 to 0.9202 — and matches
        or beats the best fixed bound on `complete` without being told which it
        is. Its one weakness is chains, exactly where and why P2 said.


IN PLAIN TERMS
--------------
When the machine decides which things belong with which, it keeps a fixed number
of best matches for each thing — the same number for everything.

`g33-02` showed why that cannot work. Picture a word that names a thing: the word
turns up with the picture and with the sound, but the picture and the sound may
never turn up together. The word is a hub with several spokes; each spoke has
exactly one link, back to the word. Allow two links each and the word cannot hold
all its spokes. Allow four and every unrelated thing in the world also grabs four
links, everything joins to everything, and the answer is one enormous blob.

The fix is obvious once stated: **stop using one number for everything.** Let each
thing look at its own list of matches and cut where the scores fall off a cliff.
A hub's list has a cliff after its spokes; a spoke's has one after its single
partner. Same rule, different answers, no number chosen by hand.

**The reason to doubt it is not a hunch, it is a measurement this project already
has.** A cliff rule needs a cliff, and note 058 looked at real language and found
a gentle slope instead — the biggest drop was 0.059 where the designed task drops
0.424, and the profile was never split in two. Worse, on a perfectly even slope
the rule's answer is decided by floating-point rounding, which a test now asserts.

So this world is designed to have a cliff, and a pass here means the rule works
**where there is a cliff** and means nothing about anywhere else. That caveat is
the whole reason this file says it twice.


WHAT IS BEING MEASURED
----------------------
The same three quantities as `g33-02`, so the two are directly comparable:

    f1         mean per-surface F1 against the true class. FLOOR IS 0.5
    bridged    share of same-concept pairs whose modalities NEVER co-occurred
               that ended in one class. **RECALL — reads 1.0000 under total
               collapse, so it is never read without `largest`**
    largest    biggest recovered class as a share of all surfaces

Arms: bound in {2, 3, derived}. The two fixed values are `g33-02`'s own, re-run
rather than quoted, so the comparison is like for like and not against a
remembered number.

PREDICTIONS
-----------
P1  THE ONE IT EXISTS FOR. On `star` at 4 and 5 surfaces, the derived bound
    reaches `bridged` above 0.90 **with `largest` below 0.15** — a real bridge
    and not a collapse. No fixed bound does both in `g33-02`: 2 bridges 0.3333
    and 0.1667, 3 collapses to `largest` 0.99.
    REFUTED IF it cannot, which would mean the per-surface bound does not
    resolve the tension either and `DECISIONS.md` §6's option is not the answer
    to this after all — leaving the star, and therefore a named concept, with no
    mechanism that handles it.

P2  ON `chain` THE DERIVED BOUND IS WORSE THAN FIXED `k` 2, and the reason is
    already visible in the scores. A mid-chain surface has two true neighbours
    whose conditional scores are ASYMMETRIC — a leaf neighbour is rarer, so it
    scores higher — and the biggest gap falls between the two rather than after
    them. Probed directly before this ran: surface 1 of a 5-chain scores 0.224
    for surface 0 and 0.103 for surface 2, then 0.042 for noise, so the argmax
    cuts after one.
    REFUTED IF it matches or beats fixed 2 on chains. That would be the better
    outcome and would mean the derived rule dominates rather than trading.

P3  ON `complete` the derived bound matches the BEST fixed bound at every
    surface count, without being told which that is — which is the property
    decision 167 measured on families and this re-tests on a different world.
    REFUTED IF it falls short of the best fixed value anywhere.

P4  NO CELL COLLAPSES. `largest` stays below 0.15 for the derived bound
    everywhere, because a noise surface's ranking has its cliff after very few
    partners and it therefore admits few.
    REFUTED IF any derived cell collapses, which would say the rule is not
    self-limiting and the collapse `g33-02` found at fixed `k` 3 is not about
    the bound being fixed at all.

SCORECARD
---------
  P1  REFUTED ON THE LETTER, CONFIRMED IN DIRECTION, and the letter is what a
            prediction is for. Bridging above 0.90 with no collapse was
            predicted at four AND five surfaces. At four: **0.9115**, largest
            0.0208 — held. At five: **0.8429**, which is below the bar.

            The substantive claim is not close. Fixed 2 bridges **0.1667** at
            five surfaces and fixed 3 collapses to largest 0.8962; the derived
            bound reaches 0.8429 with largest 0.0166 and lifts f1 from 0.4847 to
            **0.9202**. So the per-surface bound does resolve the tension
            `g33-02` found, and 0.90 was a number I chose without evidence.

  P2  SPLIT, and the split is the useful part. The derived bound was predicted
            WORSE than fixed 2 on chains. On `bridged` it is, and it degrades
            with length exactly as the pre-run probe said: **0.9792, 0.7344,
            0.6215** against fixed 2's flat 1.0000.

            **On f1 it is BETTER at every chain length** — 0.9938, 0.9109, 0.8470
            against 0.6864, 0.7233, 0.7835 — because fixed 2 over-links a leaf
            that needs only one partner, and the contamination costs more than
            the missed bridge does.

            So it is a genuine trade rather than a loss, and which side wins is
            a property of what is being asked: reach the far end of a chain, or
            recover clean classes. That is `DECISIONS.md` 14c's shape and both
            arms stay.

  P3  HELD, AND MORE STRONGLY THAN PREDICTED. On `complete` the derived bound
            matches the best fixed bound at 3 and 4 surfaces and **beats every
            fixed value in this grid** at 5 (1.0000 against 0.9236), without
            being told which is best. `g33-02` shows a fixed 4 also reaches
            1.0000 there — so the derived rule found the right answer at each
            size where no single fixed number does.

            This re-tests decision 167's families result on a different world
            and a different statistic, and it reproduces.

  P4  HELD, DECISIVELY, and it is the property that makes the rule usable.
            `largest` for the derived arm is between **0.0155 and 0.0208** in
            every one of the nine cells. The rule is self-limiting: a noise
            surface's ranking falls off after very few partners, so it admits
            few, and no amount of world-shape makes it chain everything
            together. Fixed 3 collapses in six cells out of nine.


WHAT THIS DOES NOT SETTLE, AND IT IS THE LARGEST CAVEAT IN THE g33 LINE
------------------------------------------------------------------------
**This world was designed to have a cliff and the rule needs one.** Note 058
measured real language co-occurrence decaying in steps of 0.02–0.03 where a
designed task falls 0.45 at once, bimodal at no setting. And `cliff` on an even
slope returns an answer decided by floating point — `[0.5, 0.4, 0.3, 0.2, 0.1]`
gives 2 while `[5, 4, 3, 2, 1]` gives 1, asserted in `test_grounding`.

So the honest statement is: **a per-surface bound resolves the hub-and-spoke
tension WHERE THE RANKING IS BIMODAL.** Whether the ranking is bimodal on data
this project did not generate is unmeasured here and measured negatively
elsewhere. The `conditional` profile being bimodal in this world was checked
before the grid ran rather than assumed, and that check is the thing to repeat on
any new stream before trusting a number from this arm.

**And the chain weakness has a stated cause that suggests a fix.** A mid-chain
surface's two true neighbours score asymmetrically because one is rarer, so the
biggest gap falls between them. A symmetric statistic, or taking the union of
each end's derived set rather than the intersection, would address it. Untried.
