Does adding machines buy capability, or only capacity?
======================================================

Script: experiments/g5_01_scaling.py
Status: RUN on Actions, 100/100 cells, converged. **G5 DOES NOT PASS AS
        MEASURED.** Machines compound at 48, 96 and 192 steps and stop
        compounding at 384. The fitted exponent of 0.69 describes a regime,
        not a law: it predicts 5.6 machines at seq 384 where 16 are not
        enough. The summariser called this a pass and was wrong.


IN PLAIN TERMS
--------------
g4-01 showed that a machine gets better the more company it has: hold one machine
at 16 slots and grow the network from 2 to 8, and that single machine goes from
0.861 to 0.949.

That sounds like scale helping, and it is easy to stop there. But the problem was
held FIXED while the network grew. A bigger team against the same puzzle will of
course do better. The question that actually matters is whether a bigger team can
take on a proportionally bigger puzzle -- and nothing so far has asked that.

There is already a reason to worry. g4-01 measured the cost of splitting the work
up, and that cost was near zero on short problems and large on long ones. If that
pattern continues, machines get less useful to each other exactly as the problems
get harder, which is the failure this gate is named for.


QUESTION
--------
G5's refutation condition, from GOALS: *the margin shrinks with scale.*

g1-10 measured the scaling law for a single unpartitioned model: the width needed
to solve MQAR grows as **SEQ_LEN^0.37**, roughly a cube root. That exponent was
measured at P=1 and is the yardstick.

So: fix each machine at 16 dimensions, grow the network, and ask how many
MACHINES are needed to hold accuracy at 0.9 as the sequence lengthens.

  per-machine width  16 (fixed)
  P                  1, 2, 4, 8, 16      -> total width 16, 32, 64, 128, 256
  seq_len            48, 96, 192, 384
  lr                 0.01, 0.02, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2
  seeds              1, 2, 3
  budget             16 epochs, checkpointed

Fit P_crossing against seq_len and compare the exponent to 0.37.

**The lr grid is five wide and deliberately spans both edges g4-01 pinned
against.** g4-01 chose 0.1 (its top) in some rows and 0.02 (its bottom) in
others, which means its optimum lay outside the grid in four of six rows. Sweeping
a parameter on every arm is not enough if the sweep does not contain the answer.


PREDICTIONS -- written before the run
--------------------------------------
1. **The exponent is WORSE than 0.37.** g4-01's penalty for eight-way splitting
   was 0.000 at seq 96 and 0.259 at seq 384 at the same width. If splitting costs
   more on longer sequences, then machines needed must grow faster than width
   needed. Guess: 0.45 to 0.6.

2. **But it is still sub-linear** -- comfortably below 1.0. Doubling the problem
   should not double the machines. If it does, the architecture has no scaling
   advantage worth the trouble and G5 fails outright.

3. **Single-machine accuracy at the crossing FALLS as the network grows.** At
   seq 96, eight-way gave one machine 0.949 -- but that was a network with plenty
   of headroom. At the crossing there is none by construction, so the pooling
   step should become less optional exactly where the network is largest. This is
   the prediction that would most complicate the g4-01 story, and it is the one
   worth wanting to be wrong about.

4. **P=1 fails at every sequence length tested.** A 16-wide model scores 0.833 at
   seq 48 and 0.559 at seq 96 (g1-13), both under 0.9. So every crossing lies
   strictly inside the grid rather than at its floor, which is the connection
   control -- a crossing pinned to the smallest P would mean the grid starts
   above the answer and no exponent can be fitted.

Most likely wrong: (1). If the exponent comes back AT 0.37, partitioning is free
with respect to scale, which is the best outcome available here and therefore the
one to distrust. **Check before believing:** that the crossings are not all
sitting at the same grid point, which would make the fit an artefact of
resolution rather than a measurement.


A PRECISION LIMIT, STATED UP FRONT
------------------------------------
Five grid points at doubling intervals locate a crossing to roughly +/-15% after
interpolation. Across an 8x range of sequence length that is about +/-0.07 on the
fitted exponent.

**So this can distinguish 0.37 from 0.6. It cannot distinguish 0.37 from 0.45.**
If the answer lands in that band the honest report is "consistent with 0.37, not
resolved", and a finer P grid would be the follow-up rather than a claim.

Writing that down now, before the number exists, because a precision limit
discovered after seeing the result is indistinguishable from an excuse.


WHAT WOULD REFUTE WHAT
-----------------------
- exponent near or above 1.0    -> G5 FAILS. Machines do not compound; this is a
                                   small-model curiosity, as GOALS puts it
- exponent 0.5 to 0.8           -> G5 passes weakly. Scale helps but partitioning
                                   taxes it, and the tax is quantified
- exponent indistinguishable
  from 0.37                     -> partitioning is free with respect to scale.
                                   Distrust, check the crossings are spread
- crossings all at one grid
  point                         -> no exponent can be read; the grid is too
                                   coarse and this measured nothing
- P=1 solves any length         -> the grid floor is above the crossing there and
                                   that length must be dropped from the fit


WHAT THIS DOES NOT SETTLE
--------------------------
Sixteen machines is not a network. Nothing here tests delay or churn at scale,
and both were only ever measured on the unpartitioned model. Nor does it touch
bandwidth: note 009's affordable region is a `d . rate` product and `rate` remains
unmeasured, so a favourable exponent here says nothing about whether the traffic
fits a home connection.


RESULT
------
3 seeds, best lr per arm, epoch 16. Pooled / one machine alone.

  seq_len 48       1m 0.850/0.850   2m 0.963/0.927   4m 0.996/0.948
                   8m 1.000/0.980  16m 1.000/0.959     crossing 1.44 machines
  seq_len 96       1m 0.673/0.673   2m 0.931/0.852   4m 0.995/0.870
                   8m 1.000/0.945  16m 1.000/0.928     crossing 1.88 machines
  seq_len 192      1m 0.296/0.296   2m 0.726/0.585   4m 0.925/0.681
                   8m 0.969/0.819  16m 0.993/0.723     crossing 3.75 machines
  seq_len 384      1m 0.074/0.074   2m 0.408/0.297   4m 0.666/0.431
                   8m 0.748/0.574  16m 0.769/0.462     NO CROSSING

  fitted exponent 0.69 over the three lengths that crossed
  learning-rate grid contained its answer: 9 of 20 arms chose an interior value


THE WALL AT 384, AND WHY IT IS THE RESULT
-------------------------------------------
The exponent is fitted on 48, 96 and 192 because those are the lengths where a
crossing exists. Extrapolated one step:

    predicted at seq 384:  5.6 machines
    actual:                more than 16, and 16 reaches only 0.769

**The power law does not survive a single step of extrapolation.** It is not a
scaling law with a worse exponent than 0.37; it is a curve fitted inside a regime
and quoted outside it.

What is happening is visible in the marginal returns at seq 384:

    1 -> 2 machines   +0.334
    2 -> 4            +0.258
    4 -> 8            +0.082
    8 -> 16           +0.021

Each doubling buys roughly a third of the previous one. Continued, that sums to
about 0.79 -- **it saturates below the 0.9 bar rather than approaching it.**
G5's refutation condition is *the margin shrinks with scale*, and at 384 steps it
shrinks to nothing.


THREE CHECKS BEFORE BELIEVING AN UNFAVOURABLE RESULT
------------------------------------------------------
An unfavourable result gets less scrutiny in this project than a favourable one,
by design. It should still get some.

1. **Is it undertraining?** This is the g1-12 trap: attention once looked
   capacity-limited at 0.252 and reached 1.000 when the budget doubled. Every arm
   here was checkpointed at epochs 8 and 16, and **no arm moved more than 0.014
   over that doubling.** The largest mover is precisely the cell in question,
   seq 384 at 16 machines, at +0.014. The plateau is real at this budget, and the
   local rule was already known to converge within an epoch (g1-07, g1-13).

2. **Does an independent sweep agree?** g4-01 measured d=128 split eight ways at
   seq 384 and got **0.741**. This sweep, different lr grid and different seeds,
   gets **0.748**. The wall reproduces.

3. **Is it partitioning, or is it the rule?** g1-13 measured the UNPARTITIONED
   local rule at seq 384: it crosses 0.9 at width 46.8 and reaches 0.994 at width
   64. Sixteen machines here total 256 dimensions.

   > **256 dimensions in one piece solve seq 384. The same 256 dimensions split
   > sixteen ways reach 0.769.** The wall is caused by the partitioning, not by
   > the underlying rule.

That third point is the finding. It is not that the local rule fails to scale --
it is that *cutting it into independent pieces* stops paying past a point that
depends on sequence length.


THE PREDICTIONS
----------------
1. **Exponent worse than 0.37 -- HELD, and understated.** Guessed 0.45-0.6;
   measured 0.69, well outside the +/-0.07 resolution stated before the run. But
   the deeper answer is that no single exponent describes this.

2. **Still comfortably sub-linear -- HELD only in the regime where it applies.**
   0.69 is sub-linear, and it is not comfortable; and past 192 steps the question
   is moot because the curve stops crossing at all.

3. **Single-machine accuracy falls as the network grows -- HELD, and more
   sharply than predicted.** It peaks at 8 machines and falls at 16 at **every
   sequence length**:

       seq 48    0.980 -> 0.959
       seq 96    0.945 -> 0.928
       seq 192   0.819 -> 0.723
       seq 384   0.574 -> 0.462

   Four for four. But the stated reasoning -- that headroom vanishes at the
   crossing -- is not the mechanism observed, since the fall happens at 16
   machines regardless of where the crossing is. The likelier account: a machine
   reads a fixed 16 rows of a memory that is growing, so its share of the signal
   falls as 16/d while the addressing improves, and past 8 machines the shrinking
   share wins. **Predicting the direction from the wrong reason is not a
   successful prediction**, and it is recorded here as a half-credit.

4. **P=1 fails at every length -- HELD.** 0.850, 0.673, 0.296, 0.074, all under
   the bar, so every crossing lies strictly inside the grid. The control passes.


THE TOOL SAID PASS
-------------------
The summariser's original verdict was *"Partitioning taxes scale: 0.69 against
0.37. G5 passes, and the tax is quantified."*

It fitted an exponent to the crossings it had and never asked whether that
exponent explained the length it had excluded. **A missing crossing is the most
informative point in the sweep -- it says the requirement ran off the end of the
grid -- and the tool silently dropped it.**

Fixed: the summariser now extrapolates its own fit to every length that failed to
cross and reports the mismatch. Run again on the same data it prints the failure.

Worth noting the second check written at the same time, a saturation heuristic
comparing the last doubling to the first, **did not fire** -- 0.021 against a
threshold of 0.334/20 = 0.0167. It was calibrated by guesswork and the guess was
slightly wrong. The held-out check is the load-bearing one, and the threshold has
been left alone rather than tuned until it fires on the case in front of it.


WHAT WOULD CHANGE THIS
-----------------------
- **More machines.** The grid stops at 16. Whether seq 384 crosses at 64 machines
  is untested, and the marginal-returns curve says probably not, but "probably
  not" from four points is a suggestion.
- **Wider machines.** Every machine here is 16 dimensions. The wall may be a
  property of that number rather than of partitioning as such -- g4-01 found the
  penalty shrinking sharply with total width, and 32-wide machines are untested.
  **This is the most likely way the result is wrong, and it is the next thing to
  run.**
- **Something other than a fixed split.** Every machine holds a contiguous block
  chosen once. Nothing here tests overlapping assignments, or letting machines
  specialise.
