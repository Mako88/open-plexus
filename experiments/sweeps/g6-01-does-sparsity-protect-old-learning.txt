Does sparsity protect old learning from new?
============================================

Script: experiments/g6_01_forgetting.py
Status: RUN on Actions, 96/96 cells. **PREDICTIONS 2 AND 3 REFUTED --
        JOHN WAS RIGHT.** Sparse keys beat dense on retained accuracy at
        widths 64, 80 and 96, by 0.06 to 0.08. Prediction 4 held for a=8 and
        a=16 but its stated MECHANISM is wrong, since a=4 helps at widths
        where its codes cannot separate. Below, the pre-run text. The headline the
        controls already produced is not about sparsity at all: **forgetting
        is a function of WIDTH.** At width 64 the model forgets everything
        (0.779 -> 0.008); at width 240 it forgets nothing (0.927 -> 0.923),
        dense either way.


IN PLAIN TERMS
--------------
Sparse codes were tried once here and came out worse, so the knob was left off
with a measurement saying not to reach for it.

That measurement asked the wrong question. It tested whether sparsity helps
inside a single problem, where lots of facts are crowded into one memory at once.
It does not: the crowding there comes from one address holding several different
values, and spreading the addresses further apart does nothing about that.

**But that is not what sparse codes are famous for.** Their classic job is
stopping *new* learning from wiping out *old* learning — which is a question about
what happens when the system moves from one body of data to another, and nothing
here has ever tested that.

The reason to expect it to work is mechanical. With dense codes every piece of
learning touches every part of the readout, so anything learned later smears over
everything learned earlier. With sparse codes each piece of learning touches only
a few parts, so two bodies of data that use different parts can coexist.


QUESTION
--------
Train on task A, then on task B, then re-test A.

  A and B share the key alphabet and the sequence structure, and use **disjoint
  value alphabets** -- A answers with the low eight values, B with the high eight.
  Nothing about the task changes; value tokens are remapped after generation.

  key_active   0 (dense), 32, 16, 8, 4
  lr           0.02, 0.05, 0.1
  seeds        1, 2, 3
  phases       8 epochs on A, then 8 epochs on B
  measured     A after A, A after B, B after B

Disjoint values are what makes forgetting possible at all: the delta rule pushes
the target row up and every other row down, so training on B actively suppresses
the rows A relies on. Shared values would leave nothing to forget.


THE TRADE, WHICH IS THE WHOLE POINT
-------------------------------------
Sparse keys are **worse at the task itself** -- 0.604 against 0.938 dense, measured
at d=64 with four machines. So this is not a search for a free win. It is a
question of whether better retention is worth worse peak accuracy, and the honest
figure of merit is **what accuracy on A survives at the end**, not how much was
lost.

A method that scores 0.95 and forgets down to 0.30 beats one that scores 0.60 and
holds 0.55. Reporting only the drop would rank them the other way round, so both
numbers are reported and the retained absolute is the one that decides.


PREDICTIONS -- written before the run
--------------------------------------
1. **Dense keys forget substantially.** Task A falls from about 0.95 to below
   0.50 after training on B. If it does not, there is no forgetting to prevent
   and this experiment measures nothing -- which is the connection control and is
   checked before dispatch.

2. **Sparse keys forget less, monotonically in sparsity.** a=4 retains the most,
   a=32 the least, dense least of all. This is the mechanism John pointed at and
   the one thing here worth being confident about.

3. **On RETAINED ACCURACY the winner is an intermediate sparsity, not the
   extreme.** Dense starts high and falls hard; a=4 starts low and holds. Guess:
   a=8 or a=16 ends highest. If dense still wins on the retained absolute despite
   forgetting most, sparsity is not worth having here even though prediction 2
   held -- and that would be the honest headline.

4. **Task B ends up fine in every arm.** B is trained last, so nothing has had a
   chance to overwrite it. If B is poor at high sparsity, the arm is simply too
   weak to learn the task and its retention number means nothing.

Most likely wrong: (3). It is the only one that requires two effects to trade off
in a particular direction, and this project's guesses about magnitudes have been
poor even when the directions held.


WHAT WOULD REFUTE WHAT
-----------------------
- dense does not forget          -> control fails; disjoint values are not
                                    producing interference and the design is
                                    wrong before any arm is compared
- sparse retains more AND ends
  higher in absolute terms       -> sparsity earns its place for task switching,
                                    exactly as John suggested, and the earlier
                                    negative result was answering a different
                                    question
- sparse retains more but ends
  LOWER                          -> the mechanism is real and the trade is bad.
                                    Report both and keep the knob off
- B is poor at high sparsity     -> that arm is too weak to be compared at all


WHAT THIS DOES NOT SETTLE
--------------------------
Two tasks, one switch. Real continual learning is many tasks in sequence, where
small per-switch losses compound. Nor does it test sparse VALUES, only sparse
keys -- values stay dense, which keeps the readout's job unchanged and isolates
the addressing.


THREE CONTROLS, AND EACH ONE CHANGED THE DESIGN
=================================================
All run before dispatch, at a total cost of about ten minutes.

**Control 1 -- disjoint values were not enough.** The first design gave A and B
disjoint VALUE alphabets and shared keys. Both dense and sparse forgot completely
(0.990 -> 0.008 and 0.781 -> 0.010). The reason is that sparsity separates
*addresses*: two tasks addressing the same keys retrieve through the same
columns, so there is nothing for sparsity to keep apart. Keys were split too.

**Control 2 -- there has to be room to separate.** With keys split, everything
still forgot. At width 64, 32 keys with 8 active dimensions each need 256 column
slots for 64 columns, so every column is shared by several tokens and the codes
are not separated at all. Sparsity cannot protect what it cannot separate, and
`a x tokens <= d` is the condition. Width 240 with `a=4` satisfies it.

**Control 3 -- and then it was width, not sparsity.** At width 240 with `a=4`,
task A survived at 0.919 of 0.929 -- 99% kept, against 1% at width 64. That looked
like sparsity working. **It was not: dense at width 240 keeps 100% too**
(0.927 -> 0.923). The protective variable is capacity. One favourable cell had
been read as a mechanism, and the control that ruled it out cost two minutes.


WHAT THE CONTROLS LEAVE, at one seed and one learning rate
------------------------------------------------------------
    width    dense kept    sparse a=4 kept
       64          0.01               0.00
       96          0.34               0.13
      128          0.68               0.80
      240          1.00               0.99

Sparse is worse at 96, better at 128, level at 240. **That is not a result** --
one seed, one rate, and the two arms cross over in the middle of the range, which
is exactly the shape that noise produces.


THE QUESTION, REVISED
----------------------
Not "does sparsity protect old learning" but **"at what width does forgetting
stop, and does sparsity move that width?"**

  width        48, 56, 64, 72, 80, 96, 112, 128
  key_active   0 (dense), 4, 8, 16
  lr           0.02, 0.05, 0.1
  seeds        1, 2, 3
  phases       8 epochs on A, 8 on B; A and B have disjoint keys AND values

96 jobs. Costed: 2.6 hours serial, worst job 3.4 minutes -- cheaper than the
first grid despite more cells, because it drops the wide rows where nothing
happens.

**A FOURTH CONTROL MOVED THE GRID.** The first version ran 64 to 240 because the
broken task showed a gradual slide (0.01, 0.34, 0.68, 1.00). On the well-posed
task the transition is far narrower -- 0.01 at width 64 and 0.99 by 128 -- so
everything from 160 up is flat ceiling and everything interesting happens in a
band the old grid sampled twice. Widths below 64 are added because the lower end
of the transition is now unlocated: 64 might not be the bottom.

**Pre-registered: the figure of merit is RETAINED ABSOLUTE accuracy on A**, not
the fraction kept. Sparse arms start lower, so a fraction favours whichever arm
learned least in the first place -- an arm that scores 0.20 and keeps all of it
would top a "kept" ranking while being useless.


PREDICTIONS -- written before the run, replacing those above
--------------------------------------------------------------
1. **Forgetting falls off with width in every arm**, and the transition is
   SHARP rather than gradual -- near-total at 64, essentially gone by 128, on the
   well-posed task. Already visible in the controls and the one thing here not in
   doubt. What is not known is where it starts: if width 48 already forgets
   everything, the lower edge is off the grid and only the upper edge is located.

2. **Sparsity does NOT move the width at which forgetting stops.** The controls
   show dense and sparse level at both ends, and the middle disagreement is one
   seed. Guess: the curves lie within seed noise of each other everywhere.

3. **On retained absolute accuracy, dense wins at every width**, because it is
   better at the task and no worse at holding on. If so, John's hypothesis is
   right about the mechanism -- sparse codes do separate learning -- and wrong
   about it mattering here, because capacity already solves the problem before
   sparsity gets a chance to.

4. **Where `a x 32 > width`, sparsity is actively harmful**, since the codes
   cannot separate but the representation is still degraded. That predicts a=16
   is worst everywhere below width 512, and a=8 worst below 256.

> **The edge control ran before dispatch and the grid brackets the transition.**
> Width 48 keeps **0.00** of task A -- total forgetting -- and width 112 keeps
> **0.91**. So the transition is fully inside the grid, with a floor row that
> forgets everything and a ceiling row that keeps nearly all of it.
>
> Prediction 1 worried that total forgetting at 48 would mean "the lower edge is
> off the grid". **That worry was misplaced and is withdrawn.** Where forgetting
> *begins* to be total does not matter; what matters is where it *stops*, and
> that is bracketed. A floor row pinned at zero is the correct shape for this
> grid, not a defect in it.

Most likely wrong: (2). If sparse arms hold on at a width where dense has already
collapsed -- somewhere in 64 to 96 now, since 128 is settled -- then sparsity buys
capacity, which is worth having precisely because capacity is the thing G5 says we
run out of.

*The evidence once offered for (2) has been withdrawn.* On the broken task sparse
a=4 appeared to beat dense at width 128, 0.80 kept against 0.68, and that was
reported as the reason to run this sweep. On the well-posed task dense wins there,
0.992 against 0.979. The hypothesis has not had a fair test; it has not had a
favourable one either.

- sparse holds at a width where
  dense has collapsed            -> sparsity buys capacity for task switching.
                                    John right, and it matters
- curves within seed noise       -> the mechanism is real and irrelevant here;
                                    width already does the job
- dense wins on retained

WHAT g7-02 CHANGED ABOUT THIS QUESTION
----------------------------------------
This sweep was designed before g7-02, and g7-02 reframes it without invalidating
it.

g7-02 showed that with selective storage, devices holding **one number** work at
any sequence length tested, in clusters of tens. So the regime this project now
cares about is not "total width 48 to 128 split four ways" -- it is **width-1
devices pooling in small clusters**.

This sweep measures forgetting at four partitions of a 48-to-128 total, which is
the regime that existed when it was written. That is still worth knowing: it
answers John's question about whether sparsity protects old learning, and it does
so in the setting where the forgetting transition actually sits.

**But the question it leaves open is sharper than the one it answers.** If tiny
devices can hold a task within a sequence and then lose it the moment a different
body of data arrives, they are not usable, and nothing here tests that. **Does a
cluster of width-1 devices forget?** is the follow-up, and it is a different
sweep rather than a wider grid on this one.

JOHN WAS RIGHT, AND THE BAND MATTERS
--------------------------------------
The earlier negative result on sparsity tested interference *within* a sequence.
John's point was that sparse codes are famous for something else -- protecting old
learning from new -- and that the within-sequence test did not cover it. It did
not, and he is right that it helps.

**Sparse keys at a=4 beat dense on retained accuracy at widths 64, 80 and 96**,
by margins well outside the seed spread. The mechanism is the one he named: a
sparse retrieved vector means each delta-rule update touches fewer readout
columns, so learning task B overwrites less of task A.

**But only in the transition band.** Below 56 everything forgets and the
differences are noise; above 112 dense already retains nearly everything and has
nothing to gain. Sparsity buys retention exactly where retention is contested.

**How much is it worth?** Dense at 80 retains 0.499 and sparse a=4 retains 0.558;
dense at 96 retains 0.745. So sparsity at a given width buys roughly a *third* of
a step up the width grid -- real, and modest. It is not a substitute for capacity.


PREDICTION 4 HELD AND ITS MECHANISM IS WRONG
----------------------------------------------
The prediction was that sparsity turns harmful where `a x tokens > width`, since
the codes cannot separate. a=8 and a=16 need 256 and 512 column-slots for at most
128 columns, and both are worse than dense at every width. So the prediction
holds.

**But a=4 needs 128 slots and wins at width 64, where only 64 exist.** Its codes
cannot separate either, and it helps anyway.

So the separation threshold is not the mechanism. What is happening is simpler and
weaker: **fewer touched columns means less overwriting even without full
separation.** Partial overlap still leaves some of task A's columns unvisited by
task B. The benefit is graded rather than switched on at a threshold.

Getting a prediction right for the wrong reason is worth recording as such,
because the reason is what gets reused.


THE TRADE, WHICH THE PRE-REGISTERED MEASURE EXISTS TO EXPOSE
--------------------------------------------------------------
Sparse arms learn task A *less well* and keep more of it:

    width 64:   dense learns 0.959, keeps 0.078
                a=4   learns 0.708, keeps 0.160

Ranked by fraction kept, a=4 looks twice as good. Ranked by what actually
survives -- the pre-registered measure -- it is ahead by 0.083, which is the
honest figure and a much smaller claim. The fraction would also have crowned a=16
at width 48, where it "kept" six times what it learned because it learned
essentially nothing (0.002 to 0.012, both noise).

**That is exactly why the measure was fixed before the run**, and it is the
second time this project has needed that discipline.


WHAT THIS DOES NOT SETTLE
--------------------------
The regime tested is four partitions of a 48-to-128 total, which is not where the
project now lives. g7-02 has since shown that with selective storage, devices of
ONE dimension work at every sequence length in clusters of tens. **Whether a
cluster of width-1 devices forgets -- and whether sparsity protects it -- is
untested**, and it is the question that decides whether tiny devices are usable
for more than one body of data.
