G3 -- what happens when a machine leaves and never comes back?
===============================================================

Script: experiments/g3_01_churn.py
Status: RUN on Actions, 36/36 cells. Predictions 1, 2 and 5 held. PREDICTION 3
        REFUTED in direction (b), and hard -- which casts doubt on the number
        g1-05 reports as the headline price of locality. See THE DOUBT below.


IN PLAIN TERMS
--------------
People close their laptops. On the hardware this project is aimed at, machines
leaving is not an accident -- it is the normal background condition, and the whole
design has been built on the assumption that any machine can vanish at any moment
and the rest carries on.

That has been a principle for the entire project and has never once been tested.
Nothing has ever left.

This is different from the last experiment, which lost individual MESSAGES. A
lost message is transient -- the next one arrives. A machine leaving takes its
share of the system with it and never comes back.

The interesting question is not just "how much damage" but "does it heal". And
there is a reason to think it might: this design keeps almost nothing permanent.
The working memory is rebuilt from scratch for every sequence, so a departing
machine does not carry away any stored memories -- there are none to carry. What
it takes is ROOM, plus whatever the readout had learned in that room.

If that is right, the damage should be predictable in advance from a curve we
already measured -- and that is the sharp version worth committing to.


QUESTION
--------
Train, remove a fraction of the machines partway through, and keep going.

  d_model=64, n_pairs=4, 6 seeds. Ablate a fraction f of the dimensions at the
  halfway point. Measure accuracy IMMEDIATELY after (before any further
  training), and again after the remaining epochs.

  f       0.0   0.125  0.25   0.375  0.5    0.75
  surviving width  64    56     48     40     32     16


PREDICTIONS -- written before the run
--------------------------------------
1. IMMEDIATE damage is severe, and much worse than the surviving width alone
   would suggest. The readout is tuned to a space that has just lost part of
   itself, so its remaining weights are calibrated against a signal that no
   longer arrives. Even f=0.125 should hurt noticeably.

2. IT RECOVERS with continued training. The readout relearns on what is left,
   and the associative memory needs no recovery at all because it is rebuilt per
   sequence.

3. THE SHARP ONE: **recovered accuracy tracks the width curve at the SURVIVING
   width.** From g1-06 at n_pairs=4:

        width   64     56*    48     40*    32     16
        acc     0.991  ~0.95  0.910  ~0.55  0.225  0.000
        (* interpolated between measured points)

   So the predictions are, after recovery:

        f=0.0    -> ~0.99
        f=0.125  -> ~0.95
        f=0.25   -> ~0.91
        f=0.375  -> ~0.55
        f=0.5    -> ~0.22
        f=0.75   -> ~0.00

   If that holds, **churn tolerance is readable straight off a curve we already
   have**, and "how many machines can we lose" becomes an arithmetic question
   rather than an experimental one. That is worth a great deal for the design.

4. Therefore tolerable churn at d_model=64 is about 25%: losing a quarter leaves
   48, which is at the crossing. Anything beyond that falls off a cliff, because
   the width curve does.

5. No catastrophic forgetting. Because only the readout persists, there is very
   little for a departing machine to destroy. The recovery in (2) should be
   quick -- most of the way back within the remaining epochs rather than needing
   a fresh start.

Most likely to be wrong: (3). Two ways it could fail, and they mean opposite
things --
   (a) recovery lands BELOW the width curve: ablating a trained model is worse
       than training that width from scratch, because the surviving dimensions
       were shaped to cooperate with ones that no longer exist. Plausible, and it
       would mean churn tolerance must be measured rather than derived.
   (b) recovery lands ABOVE the width curve: the surviving dimensions kept
       something useful from the larger model. Would be surprising and would
       suggest the model is over-provisioned at 64.


WHAT WOULD REFUTE WHAT
-----------------------
- recovery matches the width curve  -> churn tolerance is arithmetic. Best case
- recovery below the width curve    -> ablation damage is worse than starting
                                       small; tolerance must be measured
- recovery above the width curve    -> the model is over-provisioned; the
                                       crossing measured in g1-05 may be soft
- no recovery at all                -> something persistent IS being destroyed,
                                       contradicting the claim that only the
                                       readout carries across sequences
- immediate damage negligible       -> prediction 1 wrong, and the readout is
                                       more robust to losing its input space than
                                       expected


RESULT
------
6 seeds per cell, d_model=64, 8 epochs with ablation at the halfway point.

  churn  width   before  immediately  recovered  recovery
  0.000     64    0.992        0.992      0.992    -0.001
  0.125     56    0.992        0.970      0.991    +0.020
  0.250     48    0.992        0.943      0.986    +0.043
  0.375     40    0.992        0.857      0.980    +0.122
  0.500     32    0.992        0.749      0.924    +0.175
  0.750     16    0.992        0.365      0.578    +0.214

per-seed recovered at churn=0.5:   0.85 0.93 0.94 0.94 0.94 0.94
per-seed recovered at churn=0.75:  0.52 0.52 0.58 0.58 0.59 0.69


HELD
----
1. Immediate damage is real and grows with the loss: -0.02, -0.05, -0.14, -0.24,
   -0.63. The readout is calibrated against a signal that stopped arriving.

2. It recovers, at every level. Recovery grows with the damage, from +0.02 to
   +0.21 -- the more that was lost, the more relearning there is to do.

5. No catastrophic forgetting. Half the substrate can vanish and the model comes
   back to 0.924 within four epochs. Consistent with the claim that only the
   readout persists and the associative memory is rebuilt per sequence, so a
   departing machine takes capacity rather than memories.

**Losing half the machines costs 0.068 of accuracy.** For a system designed to
run on hardware that leaves without warning, that is the result C3 was written
to ask for.


PREDICTION 3 REFUTED -- RECOVERY LANDS FAR ABOVE THE WIDTH CURVE
------------------------------------------------------------------
Predicted recovered accuracy would track the from-scratch curve at the surviving
width. It does not, and the gap is enormous:

  width   from scratch (g1-06)   ablated to it   difference
     64                  0.991           0.992       +0.001
     48                  0.910           0.986       +0.076
     32                  0.225           0.924       +0.699
     16                  0.019           0.578       +0.559

At width 32 an ablated model reaches **0.924** where a model trained from scratch
at that width reaches **0.225**. Four times better. Same architecture, same total
epochs, same data.

The pre-written note called this direction (b) and said it "would be surprising
and would suggest the model is over-provisioned at 64". That reading is too mild.


THE DOUBT THIS RAISES, WHICH IS THE IMPORTANT PART
----------------------------------------------------
Only the readout learns here. `wk` and `wv` are frozen random projections, and a
random 32 of 64 random columns is statistically just 32 random columns. So an
ablated model and a from-scratch model at width 32 should be facing the *same
problem* with the same tools.

They are not getting the same answer. The difference has to come from the state
the readout was in when the machines left -- it starts warm, already trained on
those very dimensions, whereas from-scratch starts at zero.

Which suggests an uncomfortable possibility:

  **THE CROSSING MEASURED IN g1-05 AND g1-06 MAY BE A CONVERGENCE THRESHOLD
  RATHER THAN A CAPACITY THRESHOLD.**

If width 32 is *capable* of 0.92 and merely takes longer to get there from a cold
start, then "the local rule needs 4-6x the width of attention" is a statement
about optimisation speed, not about how much room the mechanism requires. The
headline number of this project would be measuring the wrong thing.

The distinguishing experiment is cheap and obvious: **train from scratch at width
32 for many more epochs and see whether it reaches 0.92 or plateaus below it.**

  plateaus low  -> capacity limit; the 4-6x price stands
  reaches 0.92  -> convergence limit; the price is about training budget, and
                   g1-05's headline needs restating

Nothing about the price of locality should be quoted until that has been run.
Recorded here rather than corrected in g1-05, because at this point it is a
doubt rather than a refutation -- and manufacturing certainty in either direction
would be the error.


AND A CONSEQUENCE THAT SURVIVES EITHER WAY
--------------------------------------------
Whatever the answer, one thing here is already useful: **a system that has been
trained and then damaged is far better off than one that started small.** The
survivors carry something, even though the only trained component is a linear
readout over frozen random features.

For the distribution story that is good news of a kind nobody planned for. A
network that grows, trains, loses machines and continues is a *different and
better* proposition than one sized for its worst-case membership -- and it argues
for over-provisioning early rather than sizing for the expected steady state.
